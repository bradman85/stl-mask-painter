What every control does

One section here for each panel of the app, in the order the panel shows them, plus the detail of the three file formats (mask JSON, the painted-shell PLY and the stencil STL) and the honest limits of each step. Every heading in the app has a small ? that opens this page at that section.

Getting started #

The mask painter runs entirely in the browser; nothing is uploaded anywhere and the model never leaves the page. It needs to be served over HTTP (ES modules and fetch), which is what node serve.mjs is for.

  1. Load a model — Open STL…, Load example model, or drop an .stl anywhere on the viewport.
  2. Paint the areas that must stay exposed: brush, region fill, or click a whole Part in one go.
  3. Press Build stencil in the Paint stencil panel and check the preview.
  4. Export stencil STL and print it. Hold the print on the figure; the painted areas are the windows.

Along the way you can save and reload the painting itself as mask JSON, and export the painted shell as PLY for use in other tools.

Top bar — loading & screenshots #

ControlWhat it does

Parsing uses three.js' STLLoader; normals in the file are ignored and recomputed from the triangles, because STL normals are frequently wrong or zero. Features (the palette) #

A "feature" is a named colour. Painting assigns a feature id to vertices, and the id — not the colour — is what the mask and the exports carry; the colour is how you see it.

ControlWhat it does
Feature row Click to make it the current feature (what the brush, fill and Part tools paint with). The number on the right is how many vertices currently carry that feature. The row turns dim when the feature is hidden from view.
◉ / ◌mini button, left of ✕ Hides or shows that feature's paint in the viewport. Hiding is a view setting: it changes nothing about the mask, what gets exported, or what the stencil turns into a window. Useful to see a small feature underneath a big one.
✕ Removes the feature from the palette and clears its paint from the mask. It asks first when the feature has paint, and the whole thing is one undo step (Ctrl+Z brings both the feature and its paint back if nothing else was painted in between).
Colour swatch Colour used for the next feature you add. Changing it does not recolour existing features.
New feature name Name for the next feature; Enter adds it. Empty names become "Feature N".
Add Appends the feature with the next free id (1…32,000; id 0 is reserved for "unpainted") and selects it. Ids are never reused inside a session, so an exported JSON keeps pointing at the right features.

Default palette

Eyes #4ea1ff, Pupils #111820, Mouth #ff4d6d, Cheeks #ff8fb1, Ears #b06bff, Head #ffd23f, Body #3ddc84, Arms #2fd0c5, Legs #ff9f45, Markings #ff6bd6. Press 1…9 to switch to the first nine.

What a feature does when the stencil is built

Nothing, directly: the window is everything painted, whatever feature it belongs to. Features exist so you can paint several areas in different colours, see them apart, count them, and clear them one at a time. Two features painted next to each other become a single window, joined at the seam between them.

Paint #

Five tools, all painting the current feature, all undoable, all one step per stroke or click.

Tools

ToolWhat it does
Brush B Left-drag stamps a sphere of the brush radius along the path: every vertex within the radius of the hit point gets the feature. Stamps are sub-sampled while dragging (the step tracks the on-screen size of the brush), so a fast stroke stays smooth and does not paint the same vertices twenty times. The whole drag is one undo step.
A brush stamp is a sphere, so on a thin part it can reach the far side — use Part or Region fill when that matters.
Region fill F Flood-fills the connected surface from the clicked point, walking vertex to vertex over the mesh and refusing to cross a step where the surface turns by more than the seam angle. It stops at creases, at the vertex budget, and (with mirroring on) runs a second fill from the mirrored point. One click, one undo step.
Part P Paints the whole panel of the model under the cursor — see seam parts below. Click replaces the current feature's paint with that panel, Shift-click adds a second panel, Alt-click (or Ctrl-click) removes one — the only tool that reacts to a modifier click, so the modifier is not treated as "not painting" while this tool is active. Every click also lists the other panels near the cursor, which is how you select a thin or hidden one.
Erase E The brush, painting "unpainted" instead of a feature. Use it to take back part of a bad stroke without losing the rest, or to open a gap between two windows.
Pick I Eyedropper: the painted vertices near the cursor vote (weighted by distance) and the winning feature becomes current. The neighbourhood vote is deliberate — on a dense mesh the single triangle under the cursor can be unpainted while the surface around it is not.
It previews before you click. While the Pick tool is active the ring takes the colour of the feature under the cursor and the cursor line reads Pick: Eyes (click to paint with it) — or nothing painted, with a grey ring. So a click that would do nothing is visible before you make it.
A click hands control back to whatever you were painting with, and the status bar says which feature you picked and how many vertices carry it — picking the feature you already had no longer looks like a no-op. Hold Shift while clicking to stay on the Pick tool and sample several areas in a row.
Both messages distinguish the two empty cases: nothing painted here (that feature is painted elsewhere) versus nothing painted anywhere nearby (paint an area first).

Seam parts — clicking a whole panel

Hand-painting an eye or a shirt front exactly is fiddly. The Part tool cuts the model into panels first and paints the one you click, so the window is exactly that panel.

  • The panel a click selects is the one under the cursor: the ray is resolved to the triangle the cursor is on, in the model's own face order — the order the panels were cut in (sourceTriangleIndex in js/viewer.js). The pick acceleration structure indexes the mesh in its own order, so that translation is what keeps a click on an eyebrow from painting a panel somewhere else entirely.
  • A seam is an edge whose two faces meet at more than the seam angle — a crease, not a smooth curve. Faces are flood-filled without crossing seams; each connected group is a part.
  • Seam angle (3°–60°, default 8°) is the only knob. Lower cuts finer (more, smaller panels), higher merges them. The line under the slider reports how many parts that angle produces, how many are worth clicking (1,000+ faces) and how many slivers were merged.
  • The preset row under the slider is the same control with the measured sweet spots named — Face details 5°, Portrait 8° (the default), Body parts 12°, Broad shapes 25°. One click moves the slider, re-cuts the model and lights the button up, and the status bar reports the panel count and the size of the biggest panel so you can see what the cut did before you click on the model.
  • Show seams draws the seams as yellow lines, so you can see where a click will stop before you click. It needs the same segmentation, so the first time it is on you may wait about half a second.
  • Panels smaller than 0.02% of the mesh are merged into the neighbour they share the longest seam with. Without that, the default 8° cut of the bundled model gives 7,550 parts, most of them single triangles, and clicking one paints a speck. After merging: 26 panels.
  • Segmentation is cached per model and angle; everything else (painting, the stencil) uses the same cached welded mesh, so it is a one-off cost of roughly 0.4 s on the bundled model.

Measured on the bundled figure (422,658 faces) — the same angle decides both how fine the clicks are and how much of the model one click covers:

Seam anglePartsLargest panelCharacter

At 12° a click aimed at an eyebrow selects the 48% panel behind it, which is why the Part tool feels like it runs backward there; that is what the preset row is for.

The panel list — when the click selects the wrong thing

A click can only ever select the panel the ray lands on. On the bundled model that is a problem, because one panel covers 43% of the mesh and most of the front view while the face details are 0.8–2.4% of the mesh: strips a few pixels wide. A click that misses a strip by two pixels paints the panel behind it — nearly half the model — and the strip stays bare. That looks like the tool ran backward; it did not, it selected the face it hit.

So after every Part click the panel lists the panels with surface within a brush radius of the click, nearest first, each labelled with its size and how far away it is: 

Panel 1 covers 42.8% of the mesh — a near-miss on a thin panel lands here.
Panels within 3.2 units of the click; click one to select it instead:
  Part 9   8,477 faces (2.01% of the mesh) · 0.1 away
  Part 7   9,936 faces (2.35% of the mesh) · 0.1 away
  Hide this list

Output of a click between an eye and its eyebrow on the bundled figure, with the defaults: the ray landed on the 42.8% panel, and both details are on the list, one click away.

The status bar says the same thing in words, naming the smaller panel it thinks you were aiming at and the preset that would cut it out on its own: 

Selected part 1 of 26 — 180,718 faces (42.8% of the mesh), 542,154 vertices painted as “Eyes” (seams at 8°). That is 43% of the model — the detail you aimed at is probably Part 9 (8,477 faces, 2.01% of the mesh, 0.1 away): click it in the list above or take the Face details cut (5°).

Clicking one selects it with the current feature, exactly as if you had hit it. There is no pixel-hunting and no guessing: the list is a geometric query, so it also finds panels hidden behind the surface you actually hit, which no amount of zooming would.

A click can be a whole limb. If it selects more of the model than you wanted, the status bar names the smaller panel it thinks you aimed at and the finer preset that would cut it out; otherwise pick the panel you meant from the list above. If the panel is bigger than the mask material around it, the shell around it will be a band rather than a ring — see Widen the reach.
The complement is one click away. If you wanted the opposite of what you selected, Invert mask in the Mask panel swaps painted for unpainted in a single undoable step.

Radius, mirroring and the rest

ControlWhat it does
Brush radius [ ] Radius of the brush sphere in model units. The starting value is derived from the model's diagonal, so a 10 cm figure gets a sensible brush without touching the slider. The slider tops out at 16 units; [ and ] nudge it by 0.4.
Mirror across X plane M Paints the mirrored point too, about the plane x = the number beside it (defaults to the model's centre). Every tool honours it: the brush mirrors, region fill runs a second fill from the mirrored point, the Part tool paints both parts. Turn mirroring off when the model is not symmetric — the fill's mirrored run has no seed normal to test against, so it can spread further than you expect on a asymmetric mesh.
Max vertices Region fill Budget for one flood fill (default 200,000). The fill stops when it reaches it, and the status bar says it was truncated. It exists because a 180° angle on a closed model floods the entire shell, which is slow but rarely what you want.
Undo Ctrl+Z Steps back one edit: a whole brush stroke (however many stamps), a fill, one Part click, a clear, a palette removal. 40 steps are kept; older ones are dropped.
Redo Ctrl+Shift+Z Re-applies what undo took back. Painting anything new clears the redo stack.
Navigate: off Space Painting owns the left mouse button, so orbiting needs the right button (or holding Space). This button latches the left button into orbit mode for when that is more comfortable; the status text flips to Navigate: on. Releasing Space always returns to painting.

Mask #

How the painting is stored and how it is drawn — plus the buttons that take paint back and the trim that takes out what a printer cannot use.

An STL has no UVs, so there is nowhere to put a texture: the mask is per vertex. Every vertex of the model carries a feature id (0 = unpainted) and a colour, and those colours are drawn as a second copy of the model pulled slightly in front of the surface with polygonOffset, which is why paint reads as a 3D shell hugging the figure rather than a flat decal. Unpainted vertices carry alpha 0, so nothing shows through except the paint.

ControlWhat it does
Mask opacity Opacity of the paint layer. At 100% (default) the paint hides the model under it; lower values let the figure's own shading show through the paint, which is useful on dark colours. The layer's colours are unaffected — this is only how strongly the overlay is blended.
Show mask layer Hides the paint layer entirely without touching the mask. The fastest way to see what the bare model looks like.
Ghost shell (dim unpainted area) Gives unpainted vertices a faint alpha so the whole surface reads as a translucent shell and the painted regions stand out in context. Purely a view setting; it does not change the mask or the exports.
Show base model Shows or hides the original model mesh. Off is handy when you want to look at the mask alone (with the grid still on, for scale).
Show ground grid Ground plane grid, sized from the model's bounding box. Useful for judging how big a mask window is before building it.
Wireframe Draws the model's triangles as wireframe — the quickest way to see whether the mesh is fine enough for the mask edge you want. A coarse mesh gives a blocky window edge.
Hide all paint Hides every feature at once (and a second click shows them again, since it checks whether anything is visible). A view setting; the mask is untouched. With nothing painted it says so instead of doing nothing.
Invert mask Shift+I Swaps painted for unpainted: every painted vertex is released and every unpainted vertex takes the current feature. One undo step, and it is the only action here that can touch every vertex, so it reports both numbers in the status bar.
It is the answer to "I meant everything except that". A click — Part especially — can only select what it lands on, and the complement is usually the thing you wanted: paint the panel that got in the way, invert, and the window is everything else. Note that it collapses a multi-feature mask onto the current feature, because this is a single-selection invert rather than a per-feature one.
Clear feature Clears the paint of the selected feature only. Disabled — with a tooltip telling you how many vertices it would clear — when that feature has no paint. One undo step.
Clear all Wipes the whole mask. For safety it arms on the first click (the button becomes Click again to clear for four seconds) and wipes on the second; the status bar reports how many vertices went. One undo step, so Ctrl+Z brings it all back. It does not ask with a browser dialog, because dialogs are silently blocked inside some embedded browser views.
A built stencil stays where it is — clearing the paint does not remove the shell in the viewport. Discard stencil in the stencil panel is the button for that; the status bar points at it whenever you clear with a build in hand.
Trim windows under (% of the model) The size that counts as a speck, as a share of the model's diagonal — 2% by default, about 2.6 units on the bundled figure, and it runs 0–100: at 100 every window but the largest is under it. A mask is painted with a brush, so it always ends up with company: a dab beside the eye instead of on it, a stamp from a stroke that ran off the edge of the part, a speck left where a fill stopped at a seam. A window is one connected painted area of the welded surface — the same thing the stencil build calls a window. Welded because an STL repeats every vertex once per triangle, so two triangles that share an edge share no vertex index at all and only the weld makes them neighbours.
Trim windows / List windows Trim windows releases every painted window smaller than the threshold above. One undo step, like every other mask edit. Sizes are the windows' bounding boxes rather than their vertex counts, because a speck is a size on the print: the same speck is 40 vertices on a coarse mesh and 4,000 on a dense one.
The largest window is always kept, so a threshold set too high can shrink a mask to its main region but never wipe it out (the status bar says when it was spared).
A threshold can only say “smaller than this”, and only about whole windows. That leaves one shape of mask it can never help: a mask that is one connected region — two panels that touch, a stroke back and forth, a region fill — has nothing a size test can ever release, however high it is set. The status bar says so in those words rather than looking like a dead button, and List windows is the way through: it lists every painted window on the mask with its size, how much of the model that is, the features it is painted with, and two buttons. Remove releases that one window — the largest included — and Keep only releases every other window, which is how you keep one panel out of the several you painted. Both are one undo step, and the list rebuilds itself from what is left, so the numbers on screen are the numbers in the mask. The header also reports how many windows the threshold would release, which is how you choose one instead of guessing.
The list is hidden as soon as the mask changes — every row names the vertices behind it, so after another stroke the rows would describe a mask that no longer exists. Press the button again; it only costs the island walk.
Why it matters for the stencil: every painted window becomes its own window in the shell, and windows that do not touch become separate pieces — ten specks are ten windows, ten loose shells and nothing to hold them together on the print bed. Trim or remove first, then build.
Paint statsbottom line "N of M vertices painted (x% of the mask)", where M is the model's vertex count (three per triangle: 1,267,974 for the bundled model). This is the number the mask JSON and the stencil build start from.
Picking is not an edit. The Pick tool changes which feature the tools draw with; it does not change the mask, so it is not an undo step. Everything that does change the mask is.
Nothing about painting touches the model. No export, no clear and no undo changes the loaded geometry, so the same model can be repainted any number of times.

Paint stencil (STL) #

Turns the painted area into the window of a printable shell that conforms to the model. Print it, hold it on the figure, and only the painted area is exposed to the brush.

ControlWhat it does
Build stencil Runs the whole pipeline below and reports what came out. The first build also welds the model into a shared-vertex mesh (a one-off cost, cached — the status text and the panel say how long each part took). If you repaint afterwards, the panel says the part is stale and you build again; repainting does not silently invalidate the geometry you already have.
Save first if you are stopping here. The build itself is not stored anywhere: Save mask (JSON) in the Export / import panel keeps the windows and the numbers above, so you can come back later and keep adding panels.
Windows are painted islands. Every painted area that does not touch the next one becomes a window of its own, and an island too far away to bridge becomes a piece of its own — so a few stray brush dabs are a part with a few extra windows and often several loose shells. List windows and Trim windows in the Mask panel take those out before you build.
Preview Keeps a copy of the built shell in the viewport, semi-transparent and double-sided, so you can orbit around it and see the window. It is drawn under the paint layer, so painted areas keep their exact colours and you can keep painting and clearing with the preview on. With the preview off you are looking at the mask alone.
Discard stencil Throws the built shell away — the preview in the viewport, the result in the panel and the Export stencil STL it enabled — so you can start over without reloading the model. Enabled once a build has succeeded, disabled again afterwards.
It does not touch the mask. A build is a snapshot of the paint, so clearing or repainting and keeping the old shell is a fair comparison — the panel warns when the paint no longer matches the build. Use Clear all (in the Mask panel) for the paint, this button for the shell.
Reach (mm) How far the mask material extends from the painted area's boundary, measured across the surface (not through the air). Default is about 11.5% of the model's diagonal — 15 mm on the bundled figure. It is the main "how much plastic" control: small reach = a band around the window, large reach = a shell that wraps the figure.
Wall (mm) Sheet thickness away from the window: 1.19 mm by default (0.92% of the diagonal). The window edge itself is deliberately thinner (~0.4 mm, derived as half the wall) so a brush stroke meets a thin lip rather than a wall, and paint cannot wick under it.
Standoff (mm) The gap the mask leaves between the model surface and its inner face — the clearance it needs to slide into place. Default 0.19 mm on the bundled figure (0.15% of the diagonal). The build reports the gap it achieved, not just the one you asked for; see the note below.
Window inlay (mm) Mask material that overlaps the painted side of the window boundary, so brush strokes cannot creep under the window edge. 0.35 mm by default. Raising it makes the window slightly smaller than the painted area and the edge more forgiving; setting it to 0 puts the window exactly on the painted boundary.
Widen the reach until the mask is one piece On by default. If the shell comes out in more than one piece (because the painted areas are far apart, or the band cannot close around a big window), the reach is grown automatically — up to six times, in 25% steps — and the shell is rebuilt until it is a single piece or the limit is reached. Each attempt is a full build, so this is the main reason a build can take several seconds. Turn it off for a part-sized window: a band around the panel is usually what you want there, it builds in one pass, and it uses a fraction of the plastic.
Sit the exported part on the bed (Z = 0) On by default. A slicer reads Z = 0 as the plate, and an STL keeps whatever coordinates its exporter used — so a shell built around, say, the face of a figure that stands 41 mm above its own origin lands in the slicer floating 41 mm in the air, and the part has to be dragged down before it can be printed. The viewport cannot show you that, because Centre model on the grid moves the view rather than the mesh: what you see sitting on the grid is not where the file says the part is.
With the box ticked, the exported file is moved so its lowest point is Z 0. Only Z moves, and only in the copy that gets written — X and Y stay where the model has them (the slicer's own arrange puts the part on the plate), and the mask, the panels, the preview and the built shell are untouched, so nothing you see or paint changes. The status line always says which of the three happened: it was moved (and by how far), it was already on Z 0, or the box was unticked and the file keeps the model's own coordinates.
Untick it when the Z matters: lining the mask up against the figure in a slicer, or printing mask and model in one job at the coordinates they were authored in.
Export stencil STL Writes the built shell as a binary STL (the format is documented below) named <model>-paint-stencil.stl. Enabled once a build has succeeded; a build that was refused leaves it disabled.
It saves the mask on screen, never a stale build. A build is a snapshot, so if you have painted or cleared anything since, the export builds again first (the status bar says so) and writes that — otherwise Shift-clicking two more panels and pressing export would put last round's windows in a file with today's timestamp. If that rebuild is refused, nothing is written at all, and the status bar says why. The status line names the windows and pieces that went into the file, so the numbers can be checked against the result panel without opening it.
Result panelunder the buttons Triangle count, how many pieces the shell came out in, how many windows it has (the openings a brush can reach — one per separate painted area), the reach used, the wall thickness range it achieved, the standoff, the bounding box, whether it is watertight, the measured clearance (see below), how long each stage took, and every warning the build produced. Warnings are not decoration: they are the conditions under which the part will not do what you expect.

What the build actually does

  1. Weld. STL is a triangle soup with every vertex duplicated. The vertices are welded into a shared mesh with adjacency and smooth normals (422,658 faces → 211,331 vertices on the bundled model) and the mesh's orientation is checked so "outward" is known.
  2. Surface distance field. A geodesic (across-the-surface) distance is computed from the painted set. Geodesic matters: a straight-line distance would jump across the gap between an arm and the body and start the window on the wrong limb.
  3. Smooth. The field is smoothed — more passes inside the paint than outside — so the window edge is a smooth curve instead of following the mesh's triangles. This is why the window edge insets a little inside the painted area.
  4. Clip. The surface is cut to the band [−inlay, reach]: everything within the reach of the paint, minus the window itself.
  5. Offset and close. The clipped sheet is pushed out along its normals to the standoff height, a second copy is placed a wall thickness above it, and side walls stitch the boundary loops — one closed solid.
  6. Repair and report. A loose island of shell surrounded by the window is dropped (it would print as a sliver), and point contacts in the clipped sheet are split so no edge is shared by four triangles. Both are reported. Then the mesh is checked: every edge used by exactly two triangles, consistent winding, positive volume.

Clearance — does it fit onto the model?

The standoff is the gap you ask for; the clearance is the gap you get. Every build measures the real distance from each inner-face vertex to the nearest surface triangle and reports the median, the minimum and how many spots are tighter than half the standoff. Measured on the bundled figure with two eyes painted and 71,206 inner-face vertices:

Standoff askedMedian5th percentileMinimumSpots under 0.05 mm
0.10.1000.0670.002579 (0.8%)
0.2 default0.2000.1340.00376 (0.1%)
0.30.3000.1940.00537 (0.05%)

The bulk of the mask keeps exactly the standoff you ask for. The tight spots are creases sharper than the averaged vertex normal can follow: offsetting along it there would push the inner face into the model, which is worse than touching, so the offset is pulled back to the surface at those vertices instead (spread over a ring of neighbours). That is why the minimum barely improves when you raise the standoff.

A rigid mask will rub at a handful of spots. On this figure 0.1% of the inner face sits on the surface at 0.2 mm standoff. Practical readings: 0.15–0.25 mm keeps a real gap over ~99% of the part and is a good starting point for FDM; resin printers hold a smaller gap but the same crease limit applies. The fix for a stubborn crease is geometric: keep the window away from it, lower the reach so the shell stops before it, or sand those few spots after printing.
Undercuts are geometry, not a setting. A single rigid shell has to slide on and off. On a figure with arms and a head wider than its neck, a mask that reaches right around it cannot be placed at all. Keep the reach local (a shell around the face, say) or print in a flexible resin. The app guarantees and reports a whole part; whether that part is placeable is a judgement about the model.

Model & view #

ControlWhat it does
Up axis Rotates the model into the screen's Y-up world: Y up for character models (the default), Z up for CAD and printer meshes, X up otherwise. It only changes how the model is displayed and how the camera frames it — no coordinates in the mask or any export are touched.
Centre model on the grid On by default. An STL keeps whatever coordinates its exporter used, so a mesh authored inside a CAD assembly arrives hundreds of units from the origin the grid is drawn around: it hangs in space beside the grid, with nothing on screen to move it back. With this on, a loaded model is moved so its footprint centre sits on the grid's centre lines and its lowest point sits on the grid's plane (Load example model reports the distance it moved); with it off, the model stays exactly where the file puts it.
It is a view transform, like the up axis: it moves the model in the scene and never the coordinates that the mask, the panels and every export are written in, so a mask painted before or after still lines up with the original STL and the toggle can be turned off at any time. The grid follows the model's own bounding box.
Front / Back / Left / Right / Top / Home Camera presets that frame the whole model from that direction. Home is the three-quarter view the model is loaded with. Painting works from any angle, but the brush only reaches what you can see.
Navigation Right-drag orbits, middle-drag pans, the wheel zooms. Holding Space lets the left button orbit without disarming the paint tool. On a touch screen one finger paints and two fingers pan and zoom.
Status barbottom of the viewport Left: what just happened (what was painted, how many vertices, what was exported) and any warning that needs your attention. Right: the cursor's model-space position and the frame rate.

Export / import #

ButtonWhat it does
Save mask (JSON) The session file. Saves the windows you have marked up — the painted vertex list, with the palette (feature ids, names, colours) and the build settings that were in the stencil panel — as <model>-mask.json. It is the only format that can be loaded back into the app, so this is the button to press when you want to stop and carry on later, or to send a mask to someone else.
It saves what is painted now, including this round's Shift-clicked panels — unlike Export stencil STL, which is the built shell and only as current as the last build (the export rebuilds itself when the paint has moved on; see the stencil panel). Full schema below. Refuses politely (with a status message) if nothing is painted.

Mask JSON — the format

Format stl-mask, version 1. All coordinates are implicit: the file names vertices by their index in the model's triangle soup, in the order the STL lists them, so the file is only meaningful for the model it was painted on. 

{
  "format": "stl-mask",
  "version": 1,
  "model": "Sprunki Mercuy.stl",   // the file it was painted on, for reference only
  "unit": "model units",           // documentation: nothing is converted
  "vertexCount": 1267974,          // vertices in that model = 3 × its triangle count
  "features": [                    // the palette that was in use
    { "id": 1, "name": "Eyes",   "color": "#4ea1ff" },
    { "id": 2, "name": "Pupils", "color": "#111820" }
  ],
  "paintedVertices": 6814,         // pairs below / 2
  "painted": [ 12, 1, 13, 1, 14, 1, 27, 2, ... ],
  "stencil": {                     // the build settings the mask was saved with
    "coverRadius": 15, "wall": 1.19, "standoff": 0.2, "overhang": 0.35, "growIfSplit": true
  }
}
FieldMeaning
format / versionChecked on import; a file without "format": "stl-mask" is rejected with a readable message.
modelRecorded for reference. Never used to match anything, so renaming a model does not break its mask.
vertexCountThe model's vertex count when the mask was made. Import compares it against the loaded model and warns when they differ.
featuresId, name and colour for each feature. Ids are 1…32,000; id 0 is "unpainted" and is never listed.
stencilThe build settings that were in the stencil panel when the mask was saved (reach, wall, standoff, window inlay, and whether the reach may widen until the part is one piece). Optional — an older file without it still loads; with it, Open mask (JSON) puts the numbers back into the panel so a reloaded session builds the same shell. Nothing in a build is stored, only how to make it: the geometry is rebuilt from the mask.
paintedA flat list of [vertexIndex, featureId] pairs — one entry per painted vertex, nothing per unpainted vertex. This is the part that scales with how much you painted: 82,127 painted vertices is a 0.79 MB file, the whole model painted would be 11.2 MB (measured with the app's own writer).

Only painted vertices appear, so an empty mask is a valid but useless file (the app refuses to export one). Vertices that are painted after the file was written are simply absent from it.

Ready to import

The block above is annotated, so it is not valid JSON. This one is: a complete mask for a 12-triangle model (without a stencil block — that field is optional). Copy it, paste it into example-mask.json, and load it with Open mask (JSON)… to see the round trip for yourself. 

{
  "format": "stl-mask",
  "version": 1,
  "model": "example-12-triangle.stl",
  "unit": "model units",
  "vertexCount": 36,
  "features": [
    { "id": 1, "name": "Eyes", "color": "#4ea1ff" },
    { "id": 2, "name": "Pupils", "color": "#111820" }
  ],
  "paintedVertices": 4,
  "painted": [0, 1, 1, 1, 2, 1, 3, 2]
}

Loading it on the bundled figure (1,267,974 vertices) is accepted with a warning, because vertexCount does not match: the indices would land on the wrong vertices. On the model it was written for, the four listed vertices come back painted — the first three with feature 1, the fourth with feature 2.

Import mask JSON

What happens when you pick a file, in order:

  1. The JSON is parsed and validated: format must be stl-mask and painted must be an array of numbers. Anything else throws, and the status bar shows the reason — your current painting is left alone.
  2. Features are merged into the palette by id: ones you do not have are added, ones you do have keep the colour and name you already have. Nothing is removed, so importing two files with different palettes gives you the union.
  3. The vertex list is applied to the mask: every pair sets that vertex's feature id (replacing whatever was there), and the overlay is redrawn in one go.
  4. If the file's vertexCount differs from the loaded model, the status bar warns — indices then mean different vertices, so the result will be scrambled. Nothing is blocked, because a mask from a slightly re-exported mesh is often close enough to be usable as a starting point.
  5. If the file carries a stencil block, those build settings go back into the stencil panel, so the next build matches the one the file was saved from. A file without it just leaves the panel alone.
  6. The whole import is one undo step: Ctrl+Z returns the mask to what it was before (but leaves any features the import added).
Saving your place and carrying on. The mask JSON is the "save the windows I have marked up and load them back to add more" file. Save it, close the tab (or load a different model), then come back: load the model the mask was painted on, press Open mask (JSON)…, and every window is where you left it. Shift-click more panels with the Part tool, or paint with the brush, then build or press Export stencil STL — the export rebuilds from the mask as it stands, so the file always holds every window, not just the ones that were there at the last build. A built stencil is a snapshot and is not stored in the file; the panel says the shell is stale when the mask and the build disagree.
Round trip is exact. Export → import on the same model reproduces the same per-feature vertex counts; test/app-path.test.mjs exports and re-imports the mask it paints (6,814 vertices on the bundled model) and checks every vertex and id comes back unchanged.

Painted shell PLY — the format

binary_little_endian 1.0, vertices with position and RGBA colour, faces as triangles. Only triangles whose three vertices are all painted are written, and the kept vertices are repacked onto their own list, so the file is the painted shell rather than the whole model. 

ply
format binary_little_endian 1.0
comment STL mask export - per-vertex feature colours
element vertex N
property float x
property float y
property float z
property uchar red
property uchar green
property uchar blue
property uchar alpha
element face M
property list uchar uint vertex_indices
end_header
<N × (12 bytes xyz + 4 bytes RGBA)>
<M × (1 byte count = 3 + 12 bytes indices)>
DetailBehaviour
ColourThe feature's colour, converted from the palette to 0–255 per channel, so what you see in the viewport is what other tools show.
Alpha255 for painted vertices, 0 for hidden or unpainted ones — so hiding a feature and exporting gives you its triangles at alpha 0 rather than dropping them.
A shared triangleA triangle with one unpainted corner is not included, which is why the exported triangle count is a little lower than the painted area suggests. A vertex shared by painted and unpainted triangles appears once.
SizesByte size is header + 16 × vertices + 13 × faces; the app reports both counts and the file size after export.
Not for printingThis is a zero-thickness copy of the painted surface, exactly where it was painted. For something to print, build the stencil instead.

Stencil STL — the format

Binary STL: an 80-byte header (ASCII STL Mask Painter stencil, padded), a uint32 triangle count, then 50 bytes per triangle — a normal and three vertices, all float32 little-endian. Normals are recomputed from the winding on export, so they are always consistent with the geometry even though most slicers ignore them anyway.

DetailBehaviour
UnitsUntouched: the shell is in the model's own units, in the model's own coordinate frame.
Z positionThe vertices are written with the part's lowest point at Z 0 unless Sit the exported part on the bed is unticked. X and Y are never moved, so a mask still lines up with the figure if you untick it and import both into a slicer together.
WatertightThe build only reports success after checking that every edge is used by exactly two triangles with consistent winding. A refused build leaves export disabled.
SizeExactly 84 + 50 × triangles bytes — the tests assert this, re-read the file and re-check it welds back into the same watertight, same-volume solid.
OrientationWhatever up axis you are viewing with is not baked in: the mesh is written in model coordinates, so rotate it in the slicer if your printer expects Z up. Only the Z position is moved onto the plate (see above).

How it works, and what it cannot do #

Three representations of the same idea

ThingWhat it isWhere it lives
MaskA feature id per model vertex, plus coloursIn memory; saved as mask JSON
Painted shellThe painted triangles with per-vertex coloursPLY, for looking at in other tools
StencilA printable solid whose window is the painted areaBinary STL, for printing

Things worth knowing before you print

  • Resolution comes from the mesh. The mask is per vertex, so the window edge can be no finer than the model's triangles. A coarse mesh gives a visibly blocky window; the smoothing in the build hides some of it, not all.
  • The window insets. The window edge lands a couple of units inside the painted boundary (the field is smoothed so the cut is not ragged), so a thin sliver of what you painted stays covered by mask material. The Window inlay value is the deliberate part of that overlap; on top of it, expect a small inset.
  • Clearance at creases is zero, by design. See Clearance — the offset is pulled back to the surface where the surface bends too tightly for the standoff to fit. That is a geometric limit, not a setting.
  • Undercuts decide whether the print can be placed. A rigid one-piece shell cannot wrap a shape that is wider than whatever it has to pass over. Keep the reach local, or print flexibly.
  • A click selects the panel it hits — including when that is not what you aimed at. On a mesh where one panel is half the model and the details are a fraction of a percent, a near-miss paints the big panel and leaves the small one bare. The panel list and Alt-click are the fix; nothing can make a two-pixel target easy to hit.
  • One piece is preferred, not guaranteed. Far-apart painted areas, or a window larger than the band around it, give more than one shell. The app widens the reach while that helps, then says so in plain words instead of pretending.
  • Nothing is sent anywhere. Parsing, painting, segmentation and the stencil build all run in the page; there is no server-side step and no telemetry.

How it is verified #

The geometry engine is dependency-free, so the same code that runs in the page runs under Node against the bundled model. npm test runs seven suites — six on the geometry and editing, and one that checks this page against the app. What they cannot check is the interactive half — pointing, dragging, hovering — so that lives in the validation document, one item per feature with the steps to test it and the result to expect:

SuiteWhat it proves
test/stencil.test.mjs Ten painted cases (single eye, both eyes, narrow cover, a dot smaller than the inlay, a head cap, a front slab, two far-apart features, nothing painted, everything painted, and a standoff sweep). For each: every edge used exactly twice with consistent winding and outward volume, the window genuinely open, mask material in the ring around it, the inner face never both behind the surface and inside the figure, thickness inside the expected band, one connected shell or an honest warning — and the exported STL re-read and re-checked, same volume, still watertight.
test/app-path.test.mjs The app's own classes on the real model: paint with MaskLayer, build with the panel's default numbers, write the STL with the real writer, read the blob back, and probe the result (0/139 probes over the paint hit material, 2901/2910 probes in the ring do). Also cross-checks the reported clearance against an independent measurement of every inner vertex.
test/painting.test.mjs Editing behaviour: a 9-stamp stroke is one undo step, undo/redo restore the exact vertex ids, the erase brush takes back part of a stroke, Clear feature and Clear all are each one step, history is capped, and the colour buffer holds what the Mask buttons claim (painted alpha 1, hidden and cleared alpha 0) with the right upload ranges. It also covers invert (both directions, exactly mirrored counts, undo and redo) and redo after the snapshot-based edits — Clear all, Clear feature and Invert — which is where redo used to put the old image back instead of reapplying the change.
test/parts.test.mjs The seam rule on a cube where the answer is known, then on the real model: every face in one part, sizes consistent, slivers merged, a part click painting exactly its vertices, shift-click composing two panels, and a clicked panel building a stencil whose window opens over it. It also drives a real raycast through a real camera over the whole viewport: every hit must report the triangle the cursor is on, in the model's own face order — the order the panels are cut in — and the panels they can see (eyes, brows, mouth) must be the panels those rays select.
test/parts.test.mjs Also the panel-near-a-point query on a cube where the distances are known (0.62 to the four faces beside it, 1.03 to the one opposite), and on the real model: that the opening panel is 48% of the mesh, that the smallest is 0.05%, that the thin panel a click missed is on the list within a brush radius, and that picking it paints exactly that panel and leaves the giant one alone.
test/cleanup.test.mjs The tidy-up around a mask. Trimming on a made-up mesh with a known answer — three islands of different sizes, so which ones go is arithmetic rather than judgement — including that a threshold above every island still keeps the largest one, and that two sheets meeting at a single vertex stay one island. Then the bundled figure: a blob plus two stray dabs, where the trim has to release exactly the dabs, keep the blob's vertices bit for bit, land as one undo step, survive undo and redo, and find nothing to trim the second time. It also checks the arithmetic that centres an off-origin model on the grid, and that measuring it leaves the model's own coordinates — the ones the mask and the exports use — untouched.
