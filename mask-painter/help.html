What every control does

One section here for each panel of the app, in the order the panel shows them, plus the detail of the three file formats (selection JSON, the selected-shell PLY and the stencil STL) and the honest limits of each step. Every heading in the app has a small ? that opens this page at that section.

Getting started #

The mask painter runs entirely in the browser; nothing is uploaded anywhere and the model never leaves the page. It needs to be served over HTTP (ES modules and fetch), which is what node serve.mjs is for.

  1. Load a model — Open STL…, Load example model, or drop an .stl anywhere on the viewport. The app opens empty: it does not fetch the bundled example for you, so the first model in the session is the one you chose.
  2. Select the areas that must stay exposed: click a whole Part in one go, then refine it with Select faces, or drag a rectangle over a region.
  3. Press Build stencil in the Stencil (STL) panel and check the preview.
  4. Export stencil STL and print it. Hold the print on the figure; the selected areas are the windows.

Along the way you can save and reload the selection itself as selection JSON, and export the selected shell as PLY for use in other tools.

Selection — the one thing this app does #

Select the part of the model the stencil should expose. Build stencil is built from that selection and nothing else, and every tool writes the same selection.

There is one selection, and it is a set of triangles: a triangle is what the cursor is over, what a seam separates, and what a rectangle covers. Nothing is painted, there is no palette and no colours — an earlier version of this app painted feature ids onto vertices and built the stencil from the paint, which turned out to be a second, harder way of saying "these triangles".

The two tools are two ways of adding to that one set, and each can build on the other:

ToolAddsShortcut
PartEvery triangle of the seam-bounded panel under the cursor — a head, a torso, a limbP
Select modelsThe whole separate model under the cursor (the shells inside one file), for taking a stray one outS
Select facesOne triangle at a time, or a rectangle's worth of themShift+S

So the usual way through a model is: take the head with Part, switch to Select faces and take the eyes back off it a few triangles at a time, Shift-click another panel to add it, then Build stencil from what is left. Switching tools never clears the selection, because a selection is not a tool's private state — and that is the whole arrangement.

The tools, and the three modifiers

GestureWhat it does
Click Takes the panel (Part) or the triangle (Select faces) under the cursor, and makes the selection exactly that. A second click on the same thing takes it off again.
Shift+click Adds to the selection and keeps everything already selected — another panel, another triangle, another model.
Alt+click or Ctrl+click Takes what the click covers off the selection, keeping the rest. This is how a panel selected with Part is trimmed back to shape.
Drag Select faces Draws a rectangle and takes everything inside it when you release — see the rectangle. A press that does not move is still the click above.

Three modifiers mean the same thing in every tool — plain replaces, Shift adds, Alt takes off — so refining a panel with Select faces is the same gesture at a finer scale rather than a different convention. Nothing filters a modifier out before a tool sees it: that is exactly the class of bug that once made the documented "Alt-click removes a panel" do nothing at all.

The selection is drawn as an amber tint over the model's own surface, and the triangle under the cursor is outlined white, so a click is judged before it happens. Above 150,000 selected faces the tint is switched off rather than crawling — the panel's count line says so, and Selection tint is where the overlay itself is turned down.

The rectangle — everything under a drag

Selecting a large area a triangle at a time is slow, and Part takes whole panels whether the panel is inside the area you wanted or not. So in Select faces, dragging draws a rectangle over the viewport, and every triangle whose screen centre falls inside it is selected when you release. It is a screen-space selection, not a volume: the mesh's own coordinates go through the model, view and projection matrices, divided by the clip-space w, and compared with the box — so it selects exactly what you can see under it, from any camera angle.

DetailBehaviour
Only what faces you A rectangle over the face of a closed figure also covers the back of the head, whose triangles project into the same pixels. Those are culled with each triangle's own normal against the camera (the same test the GPU uses to discard back faces), and the status bar says how many were left alone, so a box over a solid part takes the surface you can see and no more.
Plain, Shift, Alt A plain drag makes the selection exactly the box's triangles; Shift-drag adds them to what was already selected; Alt-drag (or Ctrl-drag) takes them off. A rectangle is never a toggle per triangle — toggling forty thousand triangles one at a time is not a selection anybody can reason about.
One edit One drag is one undo step, however many triangles it took, and Undo takes the whole box back. A drag that selects what is already selected is not an undo step at all.
When it takes nothing A rectangle over the background, or one whose triangles all face away, says so in the status bar rather than looking like a dead tool. The drag does not need the model under the cursor to start: a box may begin on the background and be dragged over the part you want.

A box in the viewport is a box in pixels. Move the camera and the same screen rectangle covers different triangles — that is the point of a rubber band, but it does mean a selection is only as repeatable as the camera is.

Seam panels — clicking a whole panel

Selecting an eye or a shirt front exactly, a triangle at a time, is fiddly. The Part tool cuts the model into panels first and selects the one you click, so the selection is exactly that panel.

  • The panel a click selects is the one under the cursor: the ray is resolved to the triangle the cursor is on, in the model's own face order — the order the panels were cut in (sourceTriangleIndex in js/viewer.js). The pick acceleration structure indexes the mesh in its own order, so that translation is what keeps a click on an eyebrow from selecting a panel somewhere else entirely.
  • A seam is an edge whose two faces meet at more than the seam angle — a crease, not a smooth curve. Faces are flood-filled without crossing seams; each connected group is a part.
  • Seam angle (3°–60°, default 8°) is the only knob. Lower cuts finer (more, smaller panels), higher merges them. The line under the slider reports how many parts that angle produces, how many are worth clicking (1,000+ faces) and how many slivers were merged.
  • The preset row under the slider is the same control with the measured sweet spots named — Face details 5°, Portrait 8° (the default), Body parts 12°, Broad shapes 25°. One click moves the slider, re-cuts the model and lights the button up, and the status bar reports the panel count and the size of the biggest panel so you can see what the cut did before you click on the model.
  • Show seams draws the seams as yellow lines, so you can see where a click will stop before you click. It needs the same segmentation, so the first time it is on you may wait about half a second.
  • Panels smaller than 0.02% of the mesh are merged into the neighbour they share the longest seam with. Without that, the default 8° cut of the bundled model gives 7,550 parts, most of them single triangles, and clicking one selects a speck. After merging: 26 panels.
  • Segmentation is cached per model and angle; the stencil build uses the same cached welded mesh, so it is a one-off cost of roughly 0.4 s on the bundled model.

Measured on the bundled figure (422,658 faces) — the same angle decides both how fine the clicks are and how much of the model one click covers:

Seam anglePartsLargest panelCharacter
5° Face details3587k faces (21%)eyes, eyebrows and mouth each a panel of their own; the body splits into more pieces than one click should need
8° default Portrait26181k faces (43%)both eyebrows and both eyes separate; head, torso, legs, arms, feet, tail
12° Body parts21204k faces (48%)head, torso, legs, two arms, feet, plus a short tail — but the eyebrows merge into the 48% panel

The default is the coarsest cut at which the small facial panels survive as panels of their own, and it is still coarse enough that a whole torso or limb is one click. At 12° a click aimed at an eyebrow selects the 48% panel behind it, which is why the Part tool feels like it runs backward there; that is what the preset row is for.

The panel list — when the click selects the wrong thing

A click can only ever select the panel the ray lands on. On the bundled model that is a problem, because one panel covers 43% of the mesh and most of the front view while the face details are 0.8–2.4% of the mesh: strips a few pixels wide. A click that misses a strip by two pixels selects the panel behind it — nearly half the model — and the strip stays bare. That looks like the tool ran backward; it did not, it selected the face it hit.

So after every Part click the panel lists the panels with surface within a few mesh steps of the click, nearest first, each labelled with its size and how far away it is: 

Panel 1 covers 42.8% of the mesh — a near-miss on a thin panel lands here.
Panels within 3.2 units of the click; click one to select it instead:
  Part 9   8,477 faces (2.01% of the mesh) · 0.1 away
  Part 7   9,936 faces (2.35% of the mesh) · 0.1 away
  Hide this list

Output of a click between an eye and its eyebrow on the bundled figure, with the defaults: the ray landed on the 42.8% panel, and both details are on the list, one click away.

The status bar says the same thing in words, naming the smaller panel it thinks you were aiming at and the preset that would cut it out on its own: 

Selected part 1 of 26 — 180,718 faces (42.8% of the mesh), 180,718 faces added to the selection (180,718 of 422,658 selected in all, seams at 8°). That is 43% of the model — the detail you aimed at is probably Part 9 (8,477 faces, 2.01% of the mesh, 0.1 away): click it in the list above or take the Face details cut (5°).

Clicking a row adds that panel to the selection, exactly as if you had Shift-clicked it. There is no pixel-hunting and no guessing: the list is a geometric query, so it also finds panels hidden behind the surface you actually hit, which no amount of zooming would.

Invert is the answer to "I meant everything except that". A click — Part especially — can only select what it lands on, and the complement is often the thing you wanted: select the panel that got in the way, press Invert selection (Shift+I), and the selection is everything else, in one undoable step.
A click can be a whole limb. If it selects more of the model than you wanted, the status bar names the smaller panel it thinks you aimed at and the finer preset that would cut it out; otherwise pick the panel you meant from the list above. If the panel is bigger than the mask material around it, the shell around it will be a band rather than a ring — see Widen the reach.

Nothing about selecting touches the model. Every export, clear and undo describes the same loaded geometry, so the same model can be selected over any number of times. The one exception is deliberate and has its own button: Remove selected takes faces out of the model, and Restore removed puts them back.

Cleaning up the model #

An STL is not a scene. Exporters routinely write several models into one file — a figure plus the base it was cut from, a duplicate left beside it, a blob from a boolean that did not finish — and nothing in the format says they are separate objects. Two things go wrong because of it: the extra geometry gets selected and built into the window, and the part you want can end up hanging in the air, because the bed sits under the lowest point of everything in the file, stray model included. The Select models and Select faces buttons (one tool, two scopes) are how both are dealt with: pick the geometry you do not want and take it out of the model.

Whole models — the shells in the file

With Select models (S), a click picks the model under the cursor. A click that is not on the model at all clears the pick. The models are the groups of triangles joined to each other by shared edges — the same rule a slicer uses when it reports 2 objects — so two models that merely touch at a corner stay separate and a model standing inside another is a model of its own. They are listed in the panel, biggest first, with the faces and the size of each, and a click in the list picks the same thing a click in the viewport does.

ControlWhat it does
Click Picks the model under the cursor; a second click on the same one unpicks it. Shift-click picks outright (for a row of clicks that must not flip), Alt-click or Ctrl-click unpicks.
Select all Picks every face in the model — Ctrl+A. This is the fast way round: pick the lot, then click off the few you want to keep. With one model in the file it is the only way to pick the whole of it in one action.
Select none Unpicks everything (Esc does the same). Nothing else is touched — no selection, no geometry.
Invert pick Swaps what is picked for what is not: the way to say "everything except this" without clicking the rest by hand.
Count models Cuts the file into its separate models and reports them in the status bar — how many, how big the largest is, and how big the rest are. The cut is a weld of the whole mesh, so the first one takes a moment; it is cached until the geometry changes.
Remove selected Takes the selected faces out of the model. Click it twice (the label changes to "Click again to remove" for four seconds), because a browser confirmation dialog is blocked in some embedded views — the second click is the confirmation. The model cannot be left empty: a removal that would take every face is refused with the reason.
Restore removed Puts every removed face back. Removal is stored as "the file, minus these faces" rather than as an edit to the geometry, so this is exact, not an undo of an undo.

Single faces — one triangle at a time

With Select faces (Shift+S), a click picks the one triangle under the cursor and nothing else. The face under the cursor is tinted white and the picked faces yellow while this tool is up, so "click the face you can see" means something on a surface that is otherwise one unbroken grey: turn on Show seams in the Part tool's options as well if you want the panel boundaries drawn at the same time. This is the tool for geometry that is not a separate model — a spure, a chunk welded to the figure, a ring of triangles that came out wrong — and for taking one face out to see how the stencil reacts to a hole.

The pick is a filled tint, not just an outline. The first version drew the picked faces as edges, and a single picked face was invisible: three hairlines lying exactly on the surface they trace z-fight with the triangle they belong to, so a pick that worked looked like a tool that did nothing. The tint is drawn just in front of the surface (a polygon offset, no depth write) and is still depth-tested, so it covers the faces you can see of the pick and stays behind the model where the pick is on the far side, and it is drawn whatever tool is in hand — a pick survives switching tools, so its tint has to survive with it. It is drawn as long as the pick is under 150,000 faces (a box drag over a figure picks tens of thousands, and a pick that draws nothing reads as a tool that did nothing); above that the panel says the pick is too big to tint and reports the count instead.
A selection is what the stencil is built from. There is nothing to convert first: Build stencil uses the selected triangles directly, and the panel's count line reports the two numbers that matter — how many of the model's faces are selected, and how many corners of the file's soup that is, which is what the builder consumes. Selecting a region and pressing Build stencil is the whole workflow.
Faces are the file's own triangles. A dense scan has hundreds of thousands of them, so selecting a region a triangle at a time is a job for a few triangles, not for a whole area — the rectangle and the Part tool are the region tools. Part takes one whole seam-bounded panel per click; the rectangle takes what you can see inside it.

What a removal does

  • The selection survives the cut. Every surviving face is carried over through a face map — what was selected and stayed selected is still selected, only renumbered — and the undo history is renumbered with it, so Ctrl+Z keeps working across a removal. Taking a stray model out and then building what is left is two moves, not three.
  • The model is re-measured and re-placed. The bounding box, the fit, the camera and the grid are taken again from what is left, so the surviving model stands on the bed even if it was previously standing on top of a model you have just removed. The status bar says where the bed ended up: The bed is under the surviving model's lowest point (0.00).
  • The stencil and the panels start again. The built stencil, the seam parts and the welded surface all describe the old geometry, so they are discarded rather than left saying something about a model that is no longer loaded. The mask itself is untouched.
  • The export is the model in hand. A removed model is not selected, not welded into a stencil and not in the selected shell — but it is also still in the file on disk: nothing here rewrites your STL, and Restore removed brings it back.

The bed — why a model can load in the air

The grid is the print bed, and it is drawn on the model's own lowest point, under its own footprint — in both centring modes, so an off-origin file (one authored in a CAD assembly, hundreds of units from the origin) is never left standing beside the grid. What the grid cannot fix is which lowest point: it is the lowest point of every shell in the file, so one stray model under the figure lifts the figure off the plate by that model's height. That is what the status bar reports after a removal, and what the model list is for: pick the stray, remove it, and the part you want drops onto the bed.

Windows #

Every separate group of selected faces is one window in the built stencil — one opening. These two buttons find and drop the ones you did not mean.

The selection is drawn as an amber tint over the model: the faces you selected, and nothing else. The window a stencil ends up with is the connected part of that selection on the welded surface — two groups that do not touch become two windows, and if they are far apart the print splits into separate pieces to hold against the figure.

ControlWhat it does
Drop windows under (% of the model) The size that counts as a speck, as a share of the model's diagonal — 2% by default, about 2.6 units on the bundled figure, and it runs 0–100: at 100 every window but the largest is under it. A selection always ends up with company: a sliver of a panel the click clipped, a triangle picked by mistake, a speck left where a rectangle crossed a seam. A window is one connected selected area of the welded surface — the same thing the stencil build calls a window. Welded because an STL repeats every vertex once per triangle, so two triangles that share an edge share no vertex index at all and only the weld makes them neighbours.
Drop small windows Releases every window smaller than the threshold above. One undo step, like every other selection edit. Sizes are the windows' bounding boxes rather than their triangle counts, because a speck is a size on the print: the same speck is 40 triangles on a coarse mesh and 4,000 on a dense one.
The largest window is always kept, so a threshold set too high can shrink a selection to its main region but never wipe it out (the status bar says when it was spared).
A threshold can only say “smaller than this”, and only about whole windows. That leaves one shape of selection it can never help: a selection that is one connected region — two panels that touch, a rectangle across a limb — has nothing a size test can ever release, however high it is set. The status bar says so in those words rather than looking like a dead button, and List windows is the way through.
List windows Names every window in the selection — see below. With nothing selected there is no list to show, and the panel says so rather than opening an empty box.

The window list — removing one by name

The list is the half of the trim that a size threshold cannot be. Two panels that touch weld into a single window, so a selection built by Shift-clicking neighbours is one island and no threshold, however high, will ever release part of it — the trim only ever releases islands other than the largest. Here each window has a name, a size and its own two buttons:

ButtonWhat it does
Remove Releases the window that row names — the largest included, which is exactly the one the trim refuses. One undo step, and the list rebuilds itself from what is left.
Keep only Releases every other window, which is the way to keep one panel out of the several that were selected. Disabled when there is only one window — there is nothing else to release, and a disabled button is honest about it.
Each row shows The vertices inside the window, how many units across it is (and what share of the model's size that is) and how many triangles it came to, so a speck is recognisable as a speck.
Hide this list Dismisses it. The list describes the selection as it was when it was built, so any change to the selection hides it rather than showing rows that no longer exist — List windows rebuilds it from the selection in hand.
Nothing about a selection touches the model. No export, no trim and no undo changes the loaded geometry, so the same model can be selected over and over. Removing faces is the one edit that does, and it has its own two buttons — see Cleaning up the model.

Stencil (STL) #

Turns the selected area into the window of a printable shell that conforms to the model. Print it, hold it on the figure, and only the selected area is exposed — to the brush, in the real world.

ControlWhat it does
Build stencil Runs the whole pipeline below and reports what came out. The first build also welds the model into a shared-vertex mesh (a one-off cost, cached — the status text and the panel say how long each part took). If you change the selection afterwards, the panel says the part is stale and you build again; selecting more does not silently invalidate the geometry you already have.
Save first if you are stopping here. The build itself is not stored anywhere: Save selection (JSON) in the Export / import panel keeps the windows and the numbers above, so you can come back later and keep adding panels.
Windows are selected islands. Every selected area that does not touch the next one becomes a window of its own, and an island too far away to bridge becomes a piece of its own — so a few stray selected triangles are a part with a few extra windows and often several loose shells. List windows and Drop small windows in the Windows panel take those out before you build.
Preview Keeps a copy of the built shell in the viewport, semi-transparent and double-sided, so you can orbit around it and see the window. It is drawn under the selection tint, so the selected faces keep their tint and you can keep selecting and unselecting with the preview on. With the preview off you are looking at the mask alone.
Discard stencil Throws the built shell away — the preview in the viewport, the result in the panel and the Export stencil STL it enabled — so you can start over without reloading the model. Enabled once a build has succeeded, disabled again afterwards.
It does not touch the selection. A build is a snapshot of the selection, so dropping it or selecting more and keeping the old shell is a fair comparison — the panel warns when the selection no longer matches the build. Use Select none (or click things off) for the selection, this button for the shell.
Reach (mm) How far the mask material extends from the selected area's boundary, measured across the surface (not through the air). Default is about 11.5% of the model's diagonal — 15 mm on the bundled figure. It is the main "how much plastic" control: small reach = a band around the window, large reach = a shell that wraps the figure.
Wall (mm) Sheet thickness away from the window: 1.19 mm by default (0.92% of the diagonal). The window edge itself is deliberately thinner (~0.4 mm, derived as half the wall) so a brush (the physical one) meets a thin lip rather than a wall, and paint cannot wick under it.
Standoff (mm) The gap the mask leaves between the model surface and its inner face — the clearance it needs to slide into place. Default 0.19 mm on the bundled figure (0.15% of the diagonal). The build reports the gap it achieved, not just the one you asked for; see the note below.
Window inlay (mm) Mask material that overlaps the selected side of the window boundary, so brush strokes cannot creep under the window edge. 0.35 mm by default. Raising it makes the window slightly smaller than the selected area and the edge more forgiving; setting it to 0 puts the window exactly on the selected boundary.
Widen the reach until the window is one piece On by default. If the shell comes out in more than one piece (because the selected areas are far apart, or the band cannot close around a big window), the reach is grown automatically — up to six times, in 25% steps — and the shell is rebuilt until it is a single piece or the limit is reached. Each attempt is a full build, so this is the main reason a build can take several seconds. Turn it off for a part-sized window: a band around the panel is usually what you want there, it builds in one pass, and it uses a fraction of the plastic. A shell that stays in pieces is still printable — Sit the exported part on the bed below places every piece on the plate, so the pieces print as separate parts rather than as one hovering one.
Sit the exported part on the bed (Z = 0) On by default. A slicer reads Z = 0 as the plate, and an STL keeps whatever coordinates its exporter used — so a shell built around, say, the face of a figure that stands 41 mm above its own origin lands in the slicer floating 41 mm in the air, and the part has to be dragged down before it can be printed. The viewport cannot show you that, because Centre model on the grid moves the view rather than the mesh: what you see sitting on the grid is not where the file says the part is.
With the box ticked, the exported file is moved so its lowest point is Z 0. Only Z moves, and only in the copy that gets written — X and Y stay where the model has them (the slicer's own arrange puts the part on the plate), and the mask, the panels, the preview and the built shell are untouched, so nothing you see or select changes. The status line always says which of the three happened: it was moved (and by how far), it was already on Z 0, or the box was unticked and the file keeps the model's own coordinates.
It covers every piece, not just the lowest one. A slicer imports the shells of one STL as parts of a single object, and parts keep the coordinates they were written with — only Split to objects drops each one onto the plate. So a stencil that came out in two pieces (a window on the head and one at the feet is 2 pieces 32 mm apart) would go on hovering over the upper piece's 32 mm of air, and the option would look like it had done nothing. Each piece is moved by its own lowest Z instead, so every piece rests on Z 0 in the file and nothing has to be split or dragged; the status line names the pieces when there is more than one.
Untick it when the Z matters: lining the mask up against the figure in a slicer, or printing mask and model in one job at the coordinates they were authored in.
Export stencil STL Writes the built shell as a binary STL (the format is documented below) named <model>-paint-stencil.stl. Enabled once a build has succeeded; a build that was refused leaves it disabled.
It saves the selection on screen, never a stale build. A build is a snapshot, so if the selection has changed since, the export builds again first (the status bar says so) and writes that — otherwise Shift-clicking two more panels and pressing export would put last round's windows in a file with today's timestamp. If that rebuild is refused, nothing is written at all, and the status bar says why. The status line names the windows and pieces that went into the file, so the numbers can be checked against the result panel without opening it.
Result panelunder the buttons Triangle count, how many pieces the shell came out in, how many windows it has (the openings a paintbrush can reach in the real world — one per separate selected area), the reach used, the wall thickness range it achieved, the standoff, the bounding box, whether it is watertight, the measured clearance (see below), how long each stage took, and every warning the build produced. Warnings are not decoration: they are the conditions under which the part will not do what you expect.

What the build actually does

  1. Weld. STL is a triangle soup with every vertex duplicated. The vertices are welded into a shared mesh with adjacency and smooth normals (422,658 faces → 211,331 vertices on the bundled model) and the mesh's orientation is checked so "outward" is known.
  2. Surface distance field. A geodesic (across-the-surface) distance is computed from the selected set — every selected triangle, however small: an island with no room inside it for the window inlay is cut at its own edge rather than dropped, so a single picked face is a window of its own. Geodesic matters: a straight-line distance would jump across the gap between an arm and the body and start the window on the wrong limb.
  3. Smooth. The field is smoothed — more passes inside the selection than outside — so the window edge is a smooth curve instead of following the mesh's triangles. This is why the window edge insets a little inside the selected area.
  4. Clip. The surface is cut to the band [−inlay, reach]: everything within the reach of the selection, minus the window itself.
  5. Offset and close. The clipped sheet is pushed out along its normals to the standoff height, a second copy is placed a wall thickness above it, and side walls stitch the boundary loops — one closed solid.
  6. Repair and report. A loose island of shell surrounded by the window is dropped (it would print as a sliver), and point contacts in the clipped sheet are split so no edge is shared by four triangles. Both are reported. Then the mesh is checked: every edge used by exactly two triangles, consistent winding, positive volume.

Clearance — does it fit onto the model?

The standoff is the gap you ask for; the clearance is the gap you get. Every build measures the real distance from each inner-face vertex to the nearest surface triangle and reports the median, the minimum and how many spots are tighter than half the standoff. Measured on the bundled figure with two eyes selected and 71,206 inner-face vertices:

Standoff askedMedian5th percentileMinimumSpots under 0.05 mm
0.10.1000.0670.002579 (0.8%)
0.2 default0.2000.1340.00376 (0.1%)
0.30.3000.1940.00537 (0.05%)

The bulk of the mask keeps exactly the standoff you ask for. The tight spots are creases sharper than the averaged vertex normal can follow: offsetting along it there would push the inner face into the model, which is worse than touching, so the offset is pulled back to the surface at those vertices instead (spread over a ring of neighbours). That is why the minimum barely improves when you raise the standoff.

A rigid mask will rub at a handful of spots. On this figure 0.1% of the inner face sits on the surface at 0.2 mm standoff. Practical readings: 0.15–0.25 mm keeps a real gap over ~99% of the part and is a good starting point for FDM; resin printers hold a smaller gap but the same crease limit applies. The fix for a stubborn crease is geometric: keep the window away from it, lower the reach so the shell stops before it, or sand those few spots after printing.
Undercuts are geometry, not a setting. A single rigid shell has to slide on and off. On a figure with arms and a head wider than its neck, a mask that reaches right around it cannot be placed at all. Keep the reach local (a shell around the face, say) or print in a flexible resin. The app guarantees and reports a whole part; whether that part is placeable is a judgement about the model.

The selection JSON — the format

Format stl-mask, version 1. All coordinates are implicit: the file names vertices by their index in the model's triangle soup, in the order the STL lists them, so the file is only meaningful for the model it was made on. 

{
  "format": "stl-mask",
  "version": 1,
  "model": "Sprunki Mercuy.stl",   // the file it was made on, for reference only
  "unit": "model units",           // documentation: nothing is converted
  "vertexCount": 1267974,          // vertices in that model = 3 × its triangle count
  "features": [                    // one entry: the single selection colour
    { "id": 1, "name": "Selection", "color": "#ffc94d" }
  ],
  "paintedVertices": 6814,         // pairs below / 2
  "painted": [ 12, 1, 13, 1, 14, 1, 27, 1, ... ],
  "stencil": {                     // the build settings the mask was saved with
    "coverRadius": 15, "wall": 1.19, "standoff": 0.2, "overhang": 0.35, "growIfSplit": true
  }
}
FieldMeaning
format / versionChecked on import; a file without "format": "stl-mask" is rejected with a readable message.
modelRecorded for reference. Never used to match anything, so renaming a model does not break its mask.
vertexCountThe model's vertex count when the mask was made. Import compares it against the loaded model and warns when they differ.
featuresThe mask format's feature table. This app has exactly one feature — id 1, the selection — so the array holds one entry and every corner in painted carries id 1. It is kept so that a file saved by the earlier, painting version (several colours) still opens: the ids are read and all of them come back as the one selection. Id 0 means "not selected" and is never listed.
stencilThe build settings that were in the stencil panel when the mask was saved (reach, wall, standoff, window inlay, and whether the reach may widen until the part is one piece). Optional — an older file without it still loads; with it, Open selection (JSON)… puts the numbers back into the panel so a reloaded session builds the same shell. Nothing in a build is stored, only how to make it: the geometry is rebuilt from the mask.
paintedA flat list of [vertexIndex, featureId] pairs — one entry per selected corner of the model's soup, nothing per unselected one. This is the part that scales with the selection: 82,127 corners is a 0.79 MB file, the whole model selected would be 11.2 MB (measured with the app's own writer).

Only selected corners appear, so an empty selection is a valid but useless file (the app refuses to export one). Corners selected after the file was written are simply absent from it.

Ready to import

The block above is annotated, so it is not valid JSON. This one is: a complete selection for a 12-triangle model (without a stencil block — that field is optional). Copy it, paste it into example-mask.json, and load it with Open selection (JSON)… to see the round trip for yourself.

Loading it on the bundled figure (1,267,974 vertices) is accepted with a warning, because vertexCount does not match: the indices would land on the wrong vertices. On the model it was written for, the four listed corners come back selected, and because a selection is per triangle while the file is per corner, every triangle touching one of them is selected — the two triangles those four corners belong to.

Import a saved selection

What happens when you pick a file, in order:

  1. The JSON is parsed and validated: format must be stl-mask and painted must be an array of numbers. Anything else throws, and the status bar shows the reason — your current selection is left alone.
  2. The feature table is read for its ids only. This app has one id, so whatever the file calls its colours — one, or the ten of an older painting session — every listed corner becomes part of the same selection.
  3. The corner list becomes the selection: every triangle with a corner in the file is selected, and the tint is derived from those triangles like any other selection. A loaded window can therefore come back a rim thicker than it was saved, because the file is per corner and the selection is per triangle.
  4. If the file's vertexCount differs from the loaded model, the status bar warns — indices then mean different vertices, so the result will be scrambled. Nothing is blocked, because a mask from a slightly re-exported mesh is often close enough to be usable as a starting point.
  5. If the file carries a stencil block, those build settings go back into the stencil panel, so the next build matches the one the file was saved from. A file without it just leaves the panel alone.
  6. The whole import is one undo step: Ctrl+Z returns the selection to what it was before.
Saving your place and carrying on. The selection JSON is the "save the windows I have marked up and load them back to add more" file. Save it, close the tab (or load a different model), then come back: load the model the selection was made on, press Open selection (JSON)…, and every window is where you left it. Shift-click more panels with the Part tool, or pick a few triangles with Select faces, then build or press Export stencil STL — the export rebuilds from the selection as it stands, so the file always holds every window, not just the ones that were there at the last build. A built stencil is a snapshot and is not stored in the file; the panel says the shell is stale when the selection and the build disagree.
Round trip is exact for corners, and generous for triangles. Export → import on the same model brings back exactly the corners the file lists; every triangle touching one of them is selected, which is the reading that keeps a window whole. test/app-path.test.mjs exports and re-imports a selection on the bundled model and checks the corners come back unchanged and the selection that comes with them covers what it covered.

Selected shell PLY — the format

binary_little_endian 1.0, vertices with position and RGBA colour, faces as triangles. Only triangles whose three vertices are all selected are written, and the kept vertices are repacked onto their own list, so the file is the selected shell rather than the whole model. 

ply
format binary_little_endian 1.0
comment STL mask export - selected shell, per-vertex colour
element vertex N
property float x
property float y
property float z
property uchar red
property uchar green
property uchar blue
property uchar alpha
element face M
property list uchar uint vertex_indices
end_header
<N × (12 bytes xyz + 4 bytes RGBA)>
<M × (1 byte count = 3 + 12 bytes indices)>
DetailBehaviour
ColourThe selection's own colour (amber), converted to 0–255 per channel, so what you see in the viewport is what other tools show.
Alpha255 for selected corners, 0 for the rest — so a vertex the selection only touches appears at alpha 0 rather than being dropped.
A shared triangleA triangle with one unselected corner is not included, which is why the exported triangle count is a little lower than the selected area suggests. A vertex shared by selected and unselected triangles appears once.
SizesByte size is header + 16 × vertices + 13 × faces; the app reports both counts and the file size after export.
Not for printingThis is a zero-thickness copy of the selected surface, exactly where it was selected. For something to print, build the stencil instead.

Stencil STL — the format

Binary STL: an 80-byte header (ASCII STL Mask Painter stencil, padded), a uint32 triangle count, then 50 bytes per triangle — a normal and three vertices, all float32 little-endian. Normals are recomputed from the winding on export, so they are always consistent with the geometry even though most slicers ignore them anyway.

DetailBehaviour
UnitsUntouched: the shell is in the model's own units, in the model's own coordinate frame.
Z positionThe vertices are written with the lowest point of every piece at Z 0 — a multi-piece stencil is placed piece by piece, because a slicer keeps the shells as parts of one object and only drops them when they are split into objects — unless Sit the exported part on the bed is unticked. X and Y are never moved, so a mask still lines up with the figure if you untick it and import both into a slicer together.
WatertightThe build only reports success after checking that every edge is used by exactly two triangles with consistent winding. A refused build leaves export disabled.
SizeExactly 84 + 50 × triangles bytes — the tests assert this, re-read the file and re-check it welds back into the same watertight, same-volume solid.
OrientationWhatever up axis you are viewing with is not baked in: the mesh is written in model coordinates, so rotate it in the slicer if your printer expects Z up. Only the Z position is moved onto the plate (see above).

How it works, and what it cannot do #

One selection, three representations of it

ThingWhat it isWhere it lives
SelectionA flag per triangle of the file — what every tool writesIn memory, and as the tint on the model

Things worth knowing before you print

  • Resolution comes from the mesh. The mask is per vertex, so the window edge can be no finer than the model's triangles. A coarse mesh gives a visibly blocky window; the smoothing in the build hides some of it, not all.
  • The window insets. The window edge lands a little inside the selected boundary (the field is smoothed so the cut is not ragged), so a thin sliver of what you selected stays covered by mask material. The Window inlay value is the deliberate part of that overlap; on top of it, expect a small inset. A selection with no room inside it for the inlay — a single picked face — is cut at its own edge instead, which reaches at most one triangle's width past the selection; the build says so when it happens.
  • Clearance at creases is zero, by design. See Clearance — the offset is pulled back to the surface where the surface bends too tightly for the standoff to fit. That is a geometric limit, not a setting.
  • Undercuts decide whether the print can be placed. A rigid one-piece shell cannot wrap a shape that is wider than whatever it has to pass over. Keep the reach local, or print flexibly.
  • A click selects the panel it hits — including when that is not what you aimed at. On a mesh where one panel is half the model and the details are a fraction of a percent, a near-miss selects the big panel and leaves the small one bare. The panel list and Alt-click are the fix; nothing can make a two-pixel target easy to hit.
  • One piece is preferred, not guaranteed. Far-apart selected areas, or a window larger than the band around it, give more than one shell. The app widens the reach while that helps, then says so in plain words instead of pretending.
  • Nothing is sent anywhere. Parsing, segmentation and the stencil build all run in the page; there is no server-side step and no telemetry.
